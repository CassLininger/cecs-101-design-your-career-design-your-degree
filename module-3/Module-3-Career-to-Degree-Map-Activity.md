## Step 2 — Research Each Career Path

### Career 1

**Career Title:** Information Security Analyst

**Brief Description:**  
An Information Security Analyst helps protect a company’s computers, networks, and information from cyber threats. A normal day could involve monitoring systems, investigating suspicious activity, checking for vulnerabilities, responding to security incidents, and helping improve security policies.

**Typical Education Required:**  
A bachelor’s degree in cybersecurity, computer science, information technology, or a related field is the most common path. Experience and certifications can also help when applying for jobs.

**Key Skills Required:**

1. Critical thinking
2. Problem solving
3. Network and system monitoring
4. Risk assessment
5. Communication
6. Attention to detail
7. Basic programming and scripting
8. Incident response

O*NET also lists skills such as critical thinking, writing, monitoring, active learning, speaking, and complex problem solving for this career.

**Key Knowledge Areas Required:**  
Computer networks, operating systems, cybersecurity principles, access control, risk management, cloud security, incident response, and basic programming. Current job postings commonly mention technologies such as Linux, Splunk, Python, Azure, AWS, PowerShell, and firewall software.

**Median Annual Salary:**  
$129,180 per year nationally as of May 2025.

**Job Growth Outlook:**  
Growing very quickly. BLS projects employment for Information Security Analysts to increase **21% from 2025 to 2035**, which is much faster than the average occupation.

**Typical Career Path:**  
A person could start with a degree in cybersecurity or another technology field and gain experience through internships, help desk work, networking, system administration, or an entry-level security position. From there, they could move into an Information Security Analyst position and eventually advance into jobs such as Security Engineer, Security Architect, or cybersecurity management.

**3 specific courses or experiences that would build skills for this career:**

1. **CYBR 202 – Introduction to Cybersecurity Tools**
2. **CYBR 302 – Cybersecurity Risk Management**
3. A cybersecurity internship or security operations experience

UTK's Applied Cybersecurity program includes hands-on work with monitoring, vulnerability assessment, intrusion detection, incident response, and risk management.

### Career 2

**Career Title:** Penetration Tester

**Brief Description:**  
A Penetration Tester looks for weaknesses in computer systems by attacking them in an authorized and controlled way. They may scan networks, test websites and applications, attempt to exploit vulnerabilities, document what they were able to access, and then explain how the organization could fix the problems they found.

**Typical Education Required:**  
A bachelor's degree is a common route, although there are other ways into the field through certifications and experience. O*NET places Penetration Testers in a category that usually requires considerable preparation and technical experience.

**Key Skills Required:**

1. Critical thinking
2. Programming and scripting
3. Network security
4. Vulnerability assessment
5. Complex problem solving
6. Systems analysis
7. Technical writing
8. Attention to detail

O*NET specifically lists critical thinking, programming, systems analysis, complex problem solving, systems evaluation, and written communication among important skills for penetration testers.

**Key Knowledge Areas Required:**  
Networking, operating systems, programming, web applications, computer hardware and software, telecommunications, vulnerability testing, and cybersecurity tools. Current job postings frequently mention Python, Linux, PowerShell, Nessus, Burp Suite, Metasploit, Nmap, AWS, Azure, and Kali Linux.

**Median Annual Salary:**  
O*NET lists a median annual wage of **$116,580** for Penetration Testers, using wage data from the broader “Computer Occupations, All Other” category.

**Job Growth Outlook:**  
Growing. O*NET identifies Penetration Tester as a **Bright Outlook** and new/emerging occupation. The broader occupational category is projected to grow about **8% from 2024 to 2034**, which is faster than average.

**Typical Career Path:**  
Someone interested in penetration testing could first learn networking, programming, Linux, and general cybersecurity before moving into security work. An entry-level security analyst, network security, or vulnerability assessment position could provide experience before moving further into penetration testing. With more experience, a Penetration Tester could move into positions such as Senior Penetration Tester, Red Team Operator, Security Consultant, or Security Engineer.

**3 specific courses or experiences that would build skills for this career:**

1. **CYBR 202 – Introduction to Cybersecurity Tools**
2. **COSC 366 – Introduction to Cybersecurity**
3. Practicing in legal cybersecurity labs, CTF competitions, or a personal home lab

UTK describes CYBR 202 as including hands-on work with network scanning and vulnerability assessment, while COSC 366 covers areas such as software security, operating-system security, cryptography, and network security.

## Step 4 — Reflection

**My Reflection:**  
Out of the two careers, I think Penetration Testing is more closely aligned with what I actually want to do in cybersecurity. I like the idea of being given a system and having to figure out where its weaknesses are instead of doing the exact same thing every day. It also fits with my interest in problem solving because there may not always be an obvious answer to how a system could be exploited. One thing that surprised me was how much communication and writing is involved in both careers, because I normally think of cybersecurity as being mostly technical. A Penetration Tester still has to explain what they found and give useful recommendations instead of just finding a vulnerability and moving on. This activity also made me feel more confident about pursuing Applied Cybersecurity because a lot of the courses directly connect with the skills employers are looking for. I also realized that getting the degree by itself probably will not be enough, and I need to get more hands-on experience through internships, personal labs, and cybersecurity projects while I am still in school. One question this raised for me is what entry-level position would give me the best experience to eventually become a Penetration Tester.
